Korea

In Korea, the Mid‑Autumn Festival is called Chuseok, rooted in ancient harvest and thanksgiving traditions. It is one of Korea’s most important traditional holidays. During Chuseok, people return to their hometowns to reunite with family, perform ancestral rites, and visit graves. Traditional foods are pepared, with songpyeon (rice cakes) being the most representative. Unlike China’s mooncakes, songpyeon is the iconic festive food of Chuseok.

China

The history of the Mid-Autumn Festival in China is linked to ancient moon-worship and harvest customs. Early traditions included autumn moon sacrifices, and by the Tang and Song dynasties, moon-viewing became an important festive activity. In the Northern Song, it developed into a formal festival. Today, the Mid-Autumn Festival emphasizes “reunion,” with families gathering to admire the moon, eat mooncakes, and sometimes engage in rituals such as moon worship and lantern displays.

Japan

Japan’s moon‑viewing culture was introduced from China over a thousand years ago and evolved into Jūgoya or “Mid‑Autumn Moon.” Japanese people admire the full moon in autumn and offer tsukimi dango (moon‑viewing dumplings), pampas grass, and seasonal crops to express gratitude for nature and the harvest. Compared to China, Japan emphasizes the act of moon‑viewing itself rather than eating mooncakes.

Taiwan

The history of the Mid-Autumn Festival in Taiwan comes from traditional Chinese moon-worship and harvest customs brought by early Chinese settlers. Over time, the festival became an important occasion for families to gather, admire the full moon, and celebrate together. In modern Taiwan, traditions have developed to include eating mooncakes and pomelos, giving gifts, and especially barbecuing with family and friends, which became popular in the late 20th century. Today, the Mid-Autumn Festival in Taiwan combines traditional customs with modern celebrations, focusing on family reunion, food, and spending time together.

Vietnam

Vietnam’s Mid‑Autumn Festival, Tết Trung Thu, was influenced by Chinese traditions but developed into a festival centered on children. Streets and markets are filled with lanterns and toys, and children parade with lanterns, admire the moon, and enjoy lion dances or lantern competitions. Adults eat mooncakes and admire the moon, so the festival is often regarded as a children’s holiday.

Singapore

In Singapore, the Mid‑Autumn Festival is mainly preserved by the Chinese community, originating from moon worship and reunion traditions. The National Heritage Board notes its roots in ancient harvest thanksgiving. Today, it is celebrated by families and communities with mooncakes, pomelos, tea, moon‑viewing, and lanterns. Communities host lantern fairs, riddles, and performances, with festive decorations in places like Chinatown.

Malaysia

Malaysia’s Mid‑Autumn Festival is passed down through the Chinese community, retaining traditions of moon‑viewing, eating mooncakes, and carrying lanterns. During the festival, malls and communities often hold lantern activities, dragon and lion dances, lantern parades, and float processions, blending traditional culture with modern community celebrations.
